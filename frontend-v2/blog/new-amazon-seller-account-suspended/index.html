New Sellers

Why new Amazon seller accounts get suspended more often, and what to do first

A new account carries the least history and gets the most scrutiny at the same time. Here is why a suspension shows up so early, and the three things to do before you touch the appeal.

Likith, Head of Operations · Published August 14, 2026 · 7 min read

An account that is a few weeks or a few months old gets suspended at a noticeably higher rate than one that has been selling for two years. That is not a coincidence and it is not bad luck. It comes down to one asset an established account has that a new one simply has not built yet: history. New accounts are held to the exact same Seller Code of Conduct as established ones, just without the track record to offset a first flag.

Quick answer

New accounts get suspended more easily because Amazon has no track record to weigh a single flag against, whether that flag is a document mismatch, a fast burst of orders, or a failed verification step. The first three moves are to not open a second account, to not resubmit within hours of the first rejection, and to read the notice closely enough to identify the exact policy cited before writing a word of the appeal.

Why a fresh account gets flagged faster

Every Amazon seller account is evaluated against a mix of signals: order history, return patterns, account health metrics, verification data, and behavioral patterns compared to known bad actors. An account with two years of clean selling has thousands of data points arguing in its favor before a reviewer even opens the case. A new account has almost none. When something looks off, whether it is a shipping address that does not match a business address, a supplier invoice with a formatting issue, or a sudden spike in orders during a launch week, the system and the humans behind it have nothing to weigh it against. The same event that would be a minor note on an established account becomes the entire case on a new one.

The three notices new sellers see most often

Three suspension types account for most of what happens to accounts under about six months old.

  1. Verification failures. Amazon asks for identity documents, business documents, or a video call, and something about the submission does not match cleanly, often a name spelling, an address format, or an expired document.
  2. Section 3 suspensions. A broad notice citing the Business Solutions Agreement, usually triggered by a pattern the system flagged rather than one specific order.
  3. Velocity and related account flags. A fast ramp in order volume, or a shared device, IP address, or payment method with another account, even one you closed years ago or never actually used to sell.

If you already have a notice in hand, matching it against these three helps you understand which lane you are in before you write anything. Section 3 has its own detailed breakdown in this guide, and if the notice mentions a related account specifically, this piece covers what that actually means and what not to do next.

What Amazon is actually checking on a fresh account

Underneath the specific notice, a new account review is really asking one question: is this a real, ongoing business, or a shell set up to move product quickly and disappear. Every document you submit, every shipping pattern, every piece of information on your account either supports that you are a real operator or fails to. A supplier invoice that lists a business name slightly different from your seller account name is not automatically fraud, but it is a mismatch the reviewer has to resolve, and on a new account with no other evidence to lean on, an unresolved mismatch tends to resolve against you.

Opening a second account almost always makes it worse

It is tempting to start over rather than fight the first suspension. Amazon links accounts through device, browser, payment, and address fingerprints far more often than sellers expect, and a second account discovered mid appeal turns a fixable case into a related account violation on top of the original one.

The three things to do in the first day

Before writing anything, take these three steps in order.

  1. Do not open a second account. Whatever the temptation, a new account started while the first is under review is the single fastest way to turn a recoverable case into an unrecoverable one.
  2. Do not resubmit within hours of a rejection. A same day resubmission with no new information reads as a rushed guess, and Amazon's queue treats repeat submissions from the same case with more suspicion, not less.
  3. Read the notice for the specific policy cited, not just the general category. The notice usually names a section of the Business Solutions Agreement or a specific policy page. That exact wording tells you what evidence will actually move the case.

Why rushing the plan backfires more on a new account

An experienced seller who rushes a plan of action can sometimes lean on account history to cover for a thin explanation. A new account has no such cushion. Every claim in the plan needs to be backed by a document, a screenshot, or a specific fact, because there is no track record sitting in the background making the reviewer inclined to believe you. Sellers writing their first ever plan of action often default to explaining intentions rather than proving facts, which is the opposite of what gets read favorably. Frequently asked questions

Why do new Amazon accounts get suspended more than old ones?

New accounts have no order history, return pattern, or account health record for Amazon to weigh against a single flag, so one document mismatch or unusual order pattern carries more weight than it would on an established account.

Should I just close a suspended new account and start fresh?

No. Opening a second account while the first is under review is one of the most common causes of a related account violation, and it usually turns a fixable case into a harder one.

How fast should I respond to a new account suspension notice?

Read the full notice and identify the exact policy cited before writing anything. A same day resubmission with no new evidence is treated as a rushed guess rather than a genuine correction.

Is a new account suspension harder to appeal than an established account suspension?

It requires more documentation, not necessarily a different process. Without account history to lean on, every claim in the plan of action needs its own supporting evidence rather than relying on a track record the reviewer can already see.
