Writing your first plan of action when you have never written one before

Every plan of action guide assumes you already understand what a reviewer is looking for. Here is the version written for someone doing this for the first time, with no prior appeal to learn from.

Likith, Head of Operations · Published August 14, 2026 · 8 min read

A plan of action is not a form letter and it is not an apology. It is a structured document with three distinct parts, and Amazon's reviewers read hundreds of these a day, which means they can tell within the first few lines whether the person writing it understands what is being asked for. Those three parts map directly onto how Amazon's own Seller Code of Conduct frames a violation.

Quick answer

A plan of action needs exactly three sections in order: the specific root cause of the violation, the immediate corrective action already taken, and the preventive steps that stop it from happening again. Each section needs a concrete fact or document behind it, not a general statement of intent. Sellers writing their first plan most often skip straight to promises about the future without ever specifically naming what actually caused the issue.

The three sections, and why order matters

Amazon's own guidance describes three components: root cause, corrective action, and preventive action. Reviewers read in that order and expect the same order back. A plan that opens with promises about future behavior before establishing what actually happened reads as evasive, even when the writer has no intention of hiding anything. Getting the order right is a large part of getting read favorably.

  1. Root cause. The specific, named reason the violation happened. Not "there was a misunderstanding," but the exact fact: a supplier shipped a batch with a labeling error, a document was submitted with an outdated address, a listing was created before checking category restrictions.
  2. Corrective action. What has already been done, described as completed fact, not intention. "The listing has been removed" rather than "the listing will be removed."
  3. Preventive action. The specific process change that stops recurrence, ideally something checkable, like a new verification step before listing a product in a certain category, rather than a general promise to be more careful.

Why a template alone will not work

Searching for a plan of action template returns dozens of generic structures, and generic structures are exactly what an experienced reviewer has read a thousand times. A template gives you the shape of the three sections. It cannot give you the specific facts of your case, and the facts are what actually get a plan approved or rejected. A template filled in with vague language performs worse than a shorter plan built entirely around two or three specific, verifiable facts. This guide covers the general template structure in more depth, and this one shows worked examples for different violation categories.

What "specific" actually means in practice

Vague: "We will review our processes to prevent this from happening again." Specific: "Every invoice from this supplier will be checked against the corresponding purchase order before the shipment is listed for sale, starting immediately." The second version gives the reviewer something concrete to evaluate. It also happens to be something you can actually do, which matters because a plan of action is treated as a commitment, and an unspecific one is harder to trust precisely because it commits to nothing checkable.

Do not explain your whole business history

A first time appeal writer often tries to establish credibility by describing years of experience, other platforms sold on, or general business background. None of that addresses the specific violation cited, and padding the plan with it tends to bury the two or three facts that actually matter.

What to do if you genuinely do not know the root cause

Sometimes the honest answer is that you are not entirely sure what triggered the flag, particularly on a newer account with limited history to review. In that case, the plan should focus on what you can verify: the specific order, document, or listing named in the notice, and a corrective step addressing every plausible cause you can identify, rather than guessing at a single cause you cannot actually confirm. Reviewers can tell the difference between a seller guessing confidently at the wrong cause and one being precise about genuine uncertainty while still taking concrete corrective steps.

Before you submit

Read the plan back and check that every sentence is either a fact, a document reference, or a specific action. Remove anything that is an intention, an apology, or general context about your business that does not bear on the specific cause. A plan that is four tight paragraphs built entirely from facts beats a two page plan padded with sincerity. If you have no sales history yet to support any of this, this guide covers exactly that situation.

Frequently asked questions

What are the three parts of an Amazon plan of action?

Root cause, the specific reason the violation happened, corrective action, what has already been done, and preventive action, the specific process change that stops it recurring. Amazon expects them in that order.

Can I just use a plan of action template I found online?

A template gives you structure, but reviewers have seen every generic template many times. The facts specific to your case, not the template's wording, are what actually get a plan approved.

How long should a first plan of action be?

Long enough to cover all three sections with specific facts, and no longer. A short plan built entirely from verifiable facts outperforms a long one padded with general statements or business background.

What if I do not know the exact root cause of my suspension?

State clearly what you can verify from the notice and address every plausible cause with a corrective step, rather than guessing confidently at a single cause you cannot actually confirm.
