Section 3 triage: what to do first after the notice

One clause, many possible causes. Work the case like an investigation and let the evidence pick the cause.

Likith, Head of Operations · Published November 9, 2026 · 6 min read

A Section 3 notice names one clause of the Business Solutions Agreement and says very little else. The fastest way to move is to work the case like an investigation: collect facts first, form a theory second, write the appeal last. This guide gives you a triage checklist for the first days after the notice, so you stop guessing and start building evidence.

Quick answer

Section 3 covers many different problems, so the first job is to find which one applies to you. Review what changed on the account just before the notice, gather the paper trail for your supply chain, and write a plan that shows permanent changes, not promises.

For a general explanation of the notice itself, read our guide to responding to a Section 3 suspension. This post is the practical triage companion: what to do, in what order, and what to collect.

What does Section 3 actually cover?

Section 3 is a broad clause. In our experience the notices we see under it relate to a wide spread of issues, such as suspected dropshipping, payment or identity concerns, supplier authenticity, and unusual changes in account activity. Because one clause covers so much, the notice alone rarely tells you which one applies. That is why appeals built on a guess tend to fail.

What should you review in the days before the notice?

Start with the account's recent history. What we commonly see is that enforcement follows a change, so list everything that moved shortly before the notice:

  • Payment and bank changes. New disbursement accounts or new cards.
  • Access changes. New logins, new locations, new users or new devices.
  • Bulk updates. Large flat file uploads, price changes or new listings.
  • Supplier changes. New sources or changed invoicing.
  • Sales pattern changes. A sudden jump in orders or a new category.

Write the list with dates. A timeline often points at the trigger faster than any other step.

How do you trace the supply chain paper trail?

If your notice suggests a sourcing or fulfilment concern, you need to show that products moved from a real source to your customers through a process you control. Gather what proves each link:

  1. Supplier invoices that match your account details.
  2. Purchase orders and payment records.
  3. Carrier receipts and tracking for inbound and outbound shipments.
  4. Warehouse or dispatch records showing where stock was held.

If dropshipping is the concern, our guide to the dropshipping policy violation appeal explains what the reviewer wants to see. If the issue is documents, see our evidence checklist by violation.

Do not submit while you are still guessing

An appeal that names the wrong cause cannot succeed, and a weak first plan costs time you may not have. Finish the investigation before you write.

What does a zero defect plan look like?

Amazon looks for change that lasts. "We will do better" is a promise. "We replaced our manual receiving process with barcode verification at the dock" is a change. Your plan should state what broke, what you altered and what now prevents a repeat. Examples of permanent changes include:

  • Ending a relationship with a supplier that could not document its stock.
  • Adding a check at receiving so mismatched units are caught before listing.
  • Moving to a documented, single owner process for account access.

Only list changes you have actually made. Every claim should link to a piece of evidence.

How do you know which Section 3 trigger applies?

You rarely know from the notice alone, so compare what you collected against the common themes. If the timeline shows new payment details just before the notice, the concern may be identity or payment related. If it shows new suppliers, authenticity is a more likely theme. If sales jumped, look at what drove the spike and whether the stock behind it can be proven. In our experience, the evidence usually points in one direction once it sits side by side.

Which documents deserve the most attention?

Put your effort into the documents that answer the likely trigger, not into volume. A short, clean set that proves one point beats a large folder that proves nothing. For most Section 3 cases, we would check these first:

  • The legal entity details across your account, bank and invoices.
  • Invoices that cover the period of sales in question.
  • Tracking that shows goods moving to real customers.

Our guide to invoice requirements shows what a reviewer expects to see on each one.

What should you avoid in the first days?

  • Do not open a new account to work around the problem. It can make matters worse.
  • Do not edit listings or documents in a rush to look compliant.
  • Do not send several appeals in a row. Each one should add something new.
  • Do not argue with the notice. Answer what it implies.

Stay calm and keep records. A methodical start saves time later.

What order should you work in?

  1. Save the notice and every related message.
  2. Build the timeline of recent account changes.
  3. Collect the supply chain documents.
  4. Decide the most likely trigger, based on evidence.
  5. Make the operational fixes.
  6. Write the plan around the confirmed cause.

Frequently asked questions

Is Section 3 one specific violation?

No. It is a broad clause, and in our experience the underlying issue varies from case to case. The notice rarely states which one applies.

What should I look at first?

Build a dated timeline of account changes shortly before the notice, such as payment, access, bulk updates and supplier changes.

Should I appeal as soon as possible?

Move quickly, but do not submit until you have identified the likely cause. A plan built on a guess usually fails.

What counts as a permanent fix?

A change you have already made that prevents a repeat, such as a receiving check or ending a non compliant supplier. A promise to improve is not a fix.
