AI product photography on Amazon: what's allowed and what gets flagged.

A generated image can clean up a background in seconds. It can also add a stray detail, an exaggerated feature, or a look too close to someone else's copyrighted photo, and none of that is the tool's problem to fix.

Khushi Narwal, COO · Published June 24, 2026 · 5 min read

A proper photo studio is expensive, and AI image tools have closed a real gap for small sellers who couldn't afford one. Background removal, lighting cleanup, and lifestyle scene generation now take minutes instead of a paid shoot. The catch is that Amazon's image requirements, and the intellectual property rules that sit alongside them, don't bend for how the image was made.

What these tools are genuinely useful for

Cleaning up a background to pure white, resizing and cropping to fit Amazon's exact main image specification, and generating a handful of lifestyle scenes without booking a set are all things AI tools now do reliably. For a seller managing dozens of ASINs, that's real time saved without cutting a corner that matters.

The main image rule that catches people off guard

Amazon's main image has to sit on a pure white background, with the product filling most of the frame and no added text, graphics, or watermark. AI editing tools sometimes miss this in small ways: a background that reads as white to the eye but isn't pure white by Amazon's technical standard, or a faint artifact left behind from the generation process. It's worth checking the actual pixel values, not just how the image looks on screen.

Where a generated image crosses into real risk

  • Lifestyle scenes too close to someone else's photo. A model trained on a huge volume of existing product photography can output a scene that resembles a specific competitor's staged shot closely enough to raise a copyright concern.
  • Recognizable trademarked elements in the background. A generated scene that includes a familiar logo, character, or branded object the seller has no license to use creates the same exposure as if it had been placed there deliberately.
  • Features the product doesn't actually have. Steam rising from a product with no heating function, a screen showing a display the product doesn't include, or a size comparison that doesn't match the real dimensions all read as misleading, regardless of how the image was produced.

A generated image is still the seller's claim

An AI generated photo that shows a feature the product doesn't have, or a brand element the seller doesn't have rights to, is treated as the seller's representation. "The tool added it" isn't part of how a complaint gets resolved.

A quick check before any generated image goes live

Look at the image the way a buyer would, then the way a reviewer checking a complaint would. Does every readable detail, packaging text, a visible screen, a size marker, match the physical product exactly. Does the background meet the specific requirement for that image slot. A two minute check catches most of what causes trouble later.
