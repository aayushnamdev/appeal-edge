Genuine stock, inauthentic claim: how to make the paperwork prove it

The product is real. The documents are what failed. Here is how to audit your set before you upload.

Likith, Head of Operations · Published November 9, 2026 · 6 min read

You bought real stock from a real source, yet Amazon says the item is inauthentic and rejects your invoice. This is one of the most frustrating cases a seller faces. In our experience the product is rarely the problem. The paperwork is. This guide covers why genuine inventory still fails review and gives you an audit to run before you upload a single document.

Quick answer

Genuine stock fails when the documents do not prove it. Receipts are not commercial invoices, the supplier cannot be verified, or the chain from brand to you has a gap. Fix those three things before you upload.

Other guides cover the invoice fields and what to do without an invoice. This one has a narrower angle: you did buy genuine goods, and you need to make the documents say so.

Why does Amazon reject invoices for genuine products?

A reviewer cannot hold your stock. They can only read what you send. If the paperwork is thin, unclear or inconsistent, genuine goods look the same as doubtful ones. What we commonly see is that the product was real and the proof was weak.

Are receipts and order confirmations enough?

Usually not. Retail receipts, order confirmations, pro forma invoices and packing slips tend to lack the detail a commercial invoice carries. In our experience they are among the most common reasons a submission is sent back. A strong commercial invoice shows:

  • The supplier's full legal name, address and contact details.
  • Your legal entity name and address, matching Seller Central.
  • An invoice number and date.
  • Item descriptions with quantities that line up with the ASINs in question.

Our guide to what invoices Amazon accepts goes through each field.

Why does the supplier footprint matter?

Reviewers look at who sold you the goods. If the supplier has no verifiable address, website, phone number or business registration, the invoice has less weight. We commonly see sellers lose appeals because a supplier was real but invisible online. Before you upload, check that someone outside your business could confirm the supplier exists:

  1. A working website or listing for the company.
  2. A phone number that reaches the business.
  3. A registered business address that matches the invoice.

What if you buy from a distributor, not the brand?

Then you need to show the chain. A distributor invoice alone may not be enough, because the reviewer wants to see how the goods moved from the brand to you. Useful documents include:

  • A letter of authorisation from the brand or rights holder naming the distributor.
  • The distributor's own purchase records, where they can share them.
  • Any brand confirmation that the distributor is an approved source.

Our post on the real reasons supplier invoices are rejected shows how a missing authorisation chain plays out in practice.

Never alter or recreate a document

If a supplier's invoice is imperfect, ask them for a corrected one. Editing a document yourself risks a far more serious outcome than a rejected appeal.

What should you audit before you upload?

  1. Match the entity. Compare the company name and address on the invoice with your Seller Central legal entity page, character for character.
  2. Match the volume. Check that quantities and dates cover the sales in question. For example, if you have sold hundreds of units, one small invoice will not cover them.
  3. Check the supplier. Confirm contact details are present and verifiable.
  4. Check the chain. If you buy through a distributor, attach the authorisation.
  5. Check legibility. Send clear, complete files, not cropped screenshots.

How do you tell whether the problem is the document or the product?

Ask a simple question: could a stranger read these files and conclude the goods are genuine? If the answer is not a clear yes, the document is the weak point. A supplier invoice with a missing address, an unclear item name or a quantity far below your sales gives a reviewer no reason to accept it. In our experience, once the paperwork is repaired, the product question often resolves with it.

What should you do if the supplier is slow or unhelpful?

Ask in writing and be specific. Tell the supplier exactly which details you need on the invoice and why. Keep every message, because your request itself shows effort. If the supplier will not cooperate, speak to a specialist early about other supporting evidence, such as proof of payment and shipment records. Do not wait until your account is under pressure to start this conversation.

What small details cause the most rejections?

  • A business name with a different suffix from the one on Seller Central.
  • An address with a missing unit number or postcode.
  • Item descriptions too vague to match to an ASIN.
  • Dates that fall outside the sales window.

These look minor and they are easy to fix. They are also easy to miss when you have read the same file many times. Ask someone else to check against the account page.

How should you present the documents?

Name files clearly, group them by ASIN or by supplier, and add a short note in your plan explaining what each one proves. A tidy package is easier to approve. Frequently asked questions

Why was my genuine product called inauthentic?

In our experience the product is rarely the issue. Weak, mismatched or unverifiable paperwork leaves a reviewer unable to confirm that the stock is genuine.

Is a retail receipt acceptable?

Usually not. What we commonly see is that receipts and order confirmations lack the detail of a commercial invoice and get sent back.

Do I need a letter of authorisation?

If you buy from a distributor rather than the brand, an authorisation from the rights holder helps show the chain from brand to you.

What if my supplier will not give me a better invoice?

Do not edit documents yourself. Ask for a corrected invoice, and if that fails, speak to a specialist about other supporting evidence.
